LAYZR started with a simple observation: creators had built large communities on Twitch, Instagram, and YouTube, but the tools for rewarding those communities were fragmented. Together with Peter Krysta and Tom Lamsfuß, I co-founded the company and took on the CTO role. The product grew into a blockchain-based platform for digital collectibles, creator-fan interactions, and avatars across the three major creator platforms.

My responsibility was the full technical organisation. I led engineering from zero to more than 15 full-time employees, owned the technical vision, hired the team, and stayed close to the product. That meant moving between architecture, delivery, and the unglamorous work of keeping a growing platform stable while the feature set expanded.

We built the system on Node.js and Terraform, with AWS providing the infrastructure layer. Terraform made the environment reproducible as the team grew; Node.js let us keep the API and real-time integration work in one language across a product that had many moving parts. The platform handled integrations with Twitch, Instagram, and YouTube, and at peak handled millions of transactions per month.

The most important number was not the transaction count. It was the 100k+ monthly active users and 1,000+ active streamer partnerships that depended on the platform to run reliably. We maintained 100% uptime during critical growth periods, including major creator campaigns where a failure would have been visible to an audience in real time.

The company raised €500k in seed funding and participated in a successful second round. In 2024, LAYZR was acquired by Jodel. The deal was one of the outcomes that made the operational discipline worth it: a technically sound platform, a team that could execute, and a product that had earned a place in the market.

## What I took from it

LAYZR taught me that scaling a platform is mostly a series of decisions about what not to break. The architecture mattered, but so did hiring, prioritisation, and the ability to keep a small team moving without letting quality collapse. It also taught me to separate the parts of the product that must be correct from the parts that can be improved after launch.

The acquisition was a milestone, not a finish line. The more durable lesson was that a startup's technical choices are only useful if they support the business long enough for the market to answer back.
